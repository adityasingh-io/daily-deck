# The Format Library

24 proven forms. Tags: [R] = direct research backing (comprehension/retention/persuasion evidence) · [C] = craft-tradition backing · [C+R] = both. Length fit: info = 80-180 words, feature = 350-600.

The chief editor assigns one format per piece, chosen for what the material IS. The writer executes it as a BASEPOINT, NOT A CAGE — blend, modify, and grow beyond it when the article demands. Never two adjacent pieces in the same format; rotate literary forms (16-18) sparingly — they lose force with repetition.

## 1. Kabob [C+R]
Structure: open on one concrete person/scene/moment → nut graf (the general idea and why care) → develop with evidence/mechanism → circle back to the opening for the closing image.
Best for: features — studies with human stakes, econ trends, psych findings, historical episodes.
Why: canonical WSJ feature formula; concrete openings exploit the concreteness effect on recall.
Caution: cap the anecdote at ~15% of the piece; fails if the anecdote misrepresents the general claim.

## 2. Inverted Pyramid [C]
Structure: most important fact in sentence one → supporting details in descending importance → background last.
Best for: info — news events, data releases, "a new study found" briefs.
Why: 130-year wire standard; even skimmers retain the core.
Caution: emotionally inert — never for material whose value is mechanism or arc.

## 3. Hourglass [C+R]
Structure: 2-3 sentence news summary → pivot ("It unfolded like this…") → chronological narrative with best details in sequence.
Best for: features — events where sequence is the substance (a coup, a negotiation, a discovery).
Why: Poynter/Clark form; delivers news fast then rides the narrative memory advantage (Mar et al. 2021 meta-analysis).
Caution: needs a genuine chronology; three dull beats retold is worse than a brief.

## 4. Axioms Brief [C+R]
Structure: one-sentence lede → "Why it matters:" labeled → "The details:" 2-4 tight bullets → optional "Yes, but:" or "What's next:".
Best for: info — policy news, econ data, announcements.
Why: Axios house form; labeled headings are signaling, improving recall of main points.
Caution: telegraphic tone flattens nuance; reflexive "Yes, but" is empty both-sidesing.

## 5. Card-Stack FAQ [C+R]
Structure: 3-6 real reader questions as headers, novice→advanced → each answered in 1-3 sentences → close on the question nobody can answer yet.
Best for: either — complex ongoing stories, new concepts, "what is X and should I care".
Why: Vox explainer form; questions-before-content act as prequestions, boosting learning of answers.
Caution: strawman questions kill it; each answer must stand alone.

## 6. Refutation (Myth vs Reality) [R]
Structure: state the common belief plainly and why it's plausible → explicit "But this is wrong" → the correct account with mechanism → why the myth persists.
Best for: either — misconceptions in psych/health/econ, pop-science zombies, folk beliefs.
Why: THE best-evidenced format here — refutation-text meta-analyses show reliable misconception reduction (co-activating wrong and right idea enables revision).
Caution: don't rehearse the myth vividly and refute weakly; weak against identity-protective beliefs.

## 7. Epistemic Ledger (Known / Unknown / Contested) [C+R]
Structure: what we know, stated confidently → what we don't know → what's contested and who holds what → how we'd find out.
Best for: either — replication-troubled findings, live scientific debates, foggy breaking news. THE format for single studies.
Why: science-desk convention; explicit uncertainty doesn't erode reader trust (van der Bles 2020).
Caution: reads as a shrug if "known" isn't substantive; don't file everything under "contested".

## 8. Worked Example [R]
Structure: name the principle in one line → walk one concrete problem step by step showing the principle at each step → generalize the pattern → boundary condition.
Best for: features — econ mechanisms (auctions, adverse selection), decision heuristics, statistical concepts.
Why: worked-example effect (Sweller & Cooper): novices learn procedures better from studied steps.
Caution: only for content with an actual procedure; a wrong example teaches the error with full force.

## 9. Case Study [R]
Structure: one case in depth (a patient, a firm, a town) → the general finding it embodies → calibration: how typical is this case, honestly.
Best for: features — medical/psych findings, natural experiments, Sacks-style neurology.
Why: concreteness effect — concrete text better comprehended, more interesting, better recalled.
Caution: single cases distort base rates; the calibration beat is mandatory.

## 10. Parable [C+R]
Structure: short self-contained story (historical episode, fable, thought experiment) → one turn where meaning lands → the moral, tied explicitly to a modern context.
Best for: either — ethics, incentive problems (cobra effect, Goodhart), thought experiments, cautionary history.
Why: oldest compression form; rides the narrative memory advantage.
Caution: moralizing kills it; fortune-cookie flattening betrays the material.

## 11. Mystery (Question Lede) [C+R]
Structure: open with a genuine puzzle/anomaly → deepen: why obvious answers fail → resolve with the real explanation → what remains unexplained.
Best for: features — counterintuitive findings, paradoxes, anomalous data.
Why: information-gap theory of curiosity (Loewenstein) — a felt knowledge gap sustains reading.
Caution: payoff must exceed setup or it's clickbait; no fake suspense around settled facts.

## 12. Mini-Profile (Person as Lens) [C]
Structure: the person in one characteristic scene/detail → their central idea or struggle → friction (opposition, failure, era) → what their story reveals beyond them.
Best for: features — thinkers behind ideas, book authors, historical figures.
Why: a person gives abstract ideas a memorable schema.
Caution: hagiography; most ideas have many parents.

## 13. Appraisal (Idea Obituary) [C]
Structure: what the idea claimed at its peak → what dethroned it → what genuinely survives → verdict on the legacy.
Best for: either — dead/demoted theories (ego depletion), late thinkers, books whose reputations turned.
Why: rise-and-fall is an inherently memorable arc.
Caution: premature burial — distinguish refuted from contested.

## 14. Steelman Duel [C+R]
Structure: the question, neutral → position A at its strongest → position B, same treatment → the crux: what evidence would decide it → where the balance honestly sits.
Best for: features — competing theories, policy disputes, philosophical standoffs.
Why: two-sided refutational messages outpersuade one-sided; "consider the opposite" reduces biased assimilation.
Caution: false balance when evidence is lopsided — the last beat must say so.

## 15. Briefly Noted (Micro-Review) [C]
Structure: what the work argues, one sentence → its strongest move → where it overreaches → verdict: who should read it and why.
Best for: info — book discussions at 80-180 words; papers, essays.
Why: New Yorker/TLS in-brief tradition; the verdict beat forces evaluation over summary.
Caution: snark is the cheap exit; no evidence from the work = just vibes.

## 16. Annotated Passage [C]
Structure: a short quoted passage (2-3 sentences, clearly marked) → clause-level commentary: what it means, hides, why built that way → why this passage matters now.
Best for: features — philosophy primary texts, famous speeches, the key paragraph of a study.
Why: close-reading pedagogy; commentary models expert reading.
Caution: prefer public-domain/short excerpts; commentary that paraphrases adds nothing.

## 17. The Letter [C]
Structure: direct address (to the reader, or replying to an imagined dilemma) → the concern restated with empathy → the idea as counsel, with evidence → a charge or send-off.
Best for: features — applied psychology, ethical dilemmas, Stoic/practical philosophy (the Seneca lineage).
Why: epistolary tradition from Seneca to advice columns; direct address creates stakes and warmth.
Caution: faux intimacy grates; never fabricate a letter from a real person.

## 18. Second-Person Simulation [C+R]
Structure: place the reader inside a situation ("You run a bakery in Weimar Berlin…") → constraints and choices make the mechanism felt → name the concept just experienced → release to present-day relevance.
Best for: features — econ mechanisms (hyperinflation, bank runs), historical situations, decision biases.
Why: narrative transportation increases engagement and attitude change (Green & Brock).
Caution: misfires when "you" is assigned views the reader rejects; keep the hypothetical clearly hypothetical.

## 19. Socratic Exchange [R]
Structure: a skeptic asks the naive-but-deep question → the expert answers → the skeptic pushes the genuine weak point → concession or resolution → end on the sharpest open question.
Best for: features — philosophy problems, contested claims, findings that trigger "isn't that obvious?".
Why: vicarious-dialogue research — learners overhearing deep-question dialogues outperform monologue conditions.
Caution: the skeptic must land real punches; a strawman ruins the form.

## 20. Timeline [C+R]
Structure: through-line stated up front (what changes across the arc) → 4-7 dated beats, one sentence event + one significance each → where the arc points next.
Best for: either — history of an idea, escalating stories, a thinker's evolution.
Why: chronological order matches how readers build event models.
Caution: dates without a through-line make a chronicle, not a story.

## 21. Then vs Now (Compare-Contrast) [R]
Structure: the old state/belief → the new one → the pivot that forced change → what the contrast reveals. (Variant: A-vs-B across space — two countries, two schools, two rival books.)
Best for: either — revised consensus, policy before/after, historical parallels, paired theories.
Why: compare-contrast is a top text structure for recall when signaled; side-by-side comparison improves transfer (analogical encoding).
Caution: forced symmetry; hidden disanalogies mislead.

## 22. One Number [C+R]
Structure: lead with a single striking figure → make it graspable (per person, per day, vs a familiar quantity) → what produced it → what it does and doesn't prove.
Best for: info — data releases, econ statistics, effect sizes. The strongest short data form.
Why: Harper's Index tradition; a concrete rescaled number anchors recall.
Caution: a number without denominator, base rate, or uncertainty is propaganda.

## 23. Takeaways (Numbered List) [C+R]
Structure: one-line setup (the occasion) → 3-5 numbered takeaways, each a full assertion + 1-2 sentences support → the least expected item last.
Best for: either — multi-claim sources: reports, big books, summits, election results.
Why: chunking and signaling reduce processing load and aid recall.
Caution: listicle rot — every item must carry an argument; never pad to hit a number.

## 24. Guess First (Pretest Hook) [R]
Structure: pose a specific answerable question, invite a guess → one beat of plausible wrong answers → the reveal, explained → why intuition misfires here.
Best for: info — surprising statistics, misestimated risks, biases demonstrated on the reader.
Why: pretesting effect — guessing before learning improves retention even when the guess is wrong.
Caution: needs a genuinely surprising answer; gimmicky if overused in a feed.

## Pairing notes for the editor
Strongest research warrants (prefer when correction or durable learning is the goal): Refutation (6), Guess First (24), Worked Example (8), Socratic Exchange (19), Case Study (9), Epistemic Ledger (7). Narrative workhorses for features: Kabob (1), Hourglass (3), Mystery (11), Parable (10). Reliable info-length forms: Inverted Pyramid (2), Axioms Brief (4), One Number (22), Briefly Noted (15). Literary forms (16-18): sparingly.
